Choosing between burial and cremation

When there is no instruction to follow, and the decision has landed with you.

About three quarters of funerals in Scotland are cremations. But that statistic is no help at all when the choice is yours and the person left no instruction.

Start with what they said

Not what is written down — most people leave nothing in writing — but what they said in passing. Scatter me at the harbour. I want to be beside your father. I don’t want to be burned. Half-remembered remarks over a cup of tea are the most common basis for this decision, and they are a perfectly legitimate one.

If nobody can recall anything, that is common too, and it means you cannot get this wrong.

What might point toward burial

  • There is an existing family lair with room. This is the most frequent reason, and often decisive.
  • Their faith expects burial, or discourages cremation.
  • Someone in the family needs a place to visit. This matters more than people expect, and often more to those who thought it would not.
  • They were plainly uneasy about cremation, even if they never spelled out why.

What might point toward cremation

  • Cost. Burial is usually more expensive, largely because of the lair purchase and interment fee. This is a legitimate reason and nobody should feel awkward about it.
  • Flexibility. Ashes can be divided, kept, buried later, or scattered somewhere that meant something. Nothing has to be decided on the day.
  • The family is scattered. A grave five hundred miles from most of the family may never be visited.
  • No existing lair, and no wish to establish one.

What is reversible and what is not

Cremation cannot be undone. Burial, in practice, is very difficult to undo — exhumation requires legal authority and is rarely granted.

But the ashes decision is entirely reversible. If you choose cremation, you can keep the ashes indefinitely while you decide. Funeral directors will hold them for as long as you need, usually at no charge. There is no deadline, and a year later is a perfectly normal time to decide.

If you are stuck, this asymmetry is worth weighing: cremation defers the second decision, burial does not.

What does not matter as much as people think

Environmental impact. It is genuinely arguable both ways. Cremation uses energy; conventional burial uses land, hardwood and embalming fluid. If this matters to you, natural burial is the option with a clear answer, and a natural coffin can be used for either.

What other people will think. Nobody at the funeral will be assessing your choice. They will be thinking about the person who died.

You do not have to decide today

Nothing about this needs settling in the first days. Talk to the family, talk to your funeral director, and take the time. It is one of the few decisions in a funeral where thinking longer genuinely produces a better answer.
