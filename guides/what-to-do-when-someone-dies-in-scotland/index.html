What to do when someone dies in Scotland

The order things need to happen in, and how much of it can safely wait until tomorrow.

Almost nothing has to happen in the first few hours. That is the single most useful thing to know, and nobody tells you it at the time.

1. A doctor confirms the death

Nothing else can proceed until this is done.

At home, where the death was expected. Telephone the GP practice during surgery hours, or NHS 24 on 111 outside them. There is no need to dial 999 — an ambulance cannot change anything.

At home, suddenly or unexpectedly. Dial 999.

In hospital. The ward will do everything and put you in touch with the bereavement office.

In a care home. The home contacts the GP, and will usually contact the funeral director on your behalf if you have told them who you would like.

2. The death is reported to the Procurator Fiscal, sometimes

Sudden, unexplained, accidental or industrial deaths are reported to the Procurator Fiscal. So are deaths where no doctor can certify the cause. This is routine and does not mean anyone suspects anything.

The Fiscal may order further examination, which can add several days before the death can be registered. It is worth knowing that in advance, because the wait is hard when nobody has explained it.

3. Contact a funeral director

Whenever you are ready — that evening, or the following morning. There is no rule requiring the person to be moved within any particular time, and if family are travelling to see them at home first, that is entirely possible.

You are not committed by asking a funeral director to bring someone into their care. You can still change your mind afterwards.

4. Register the death

Within eight days, at any registration office in Scotland — not necessarily the one where the person lived or died.

You will need an appointment. Take the person’s birth certificate, marriage certificate, National Insurance number and pension details if you can find them, and your own photo identification.

You will come away with the Certificate of Registration of Death, which the funeral director needs, and you can buy full extracts — the certificates banks and insurers ask for. Most families need between four and eight, and they cost less bought at the appointment than ordered afterwards.

See our guide to registering a death in East Lothian and our directory of registration offices.

5. Use Tell Us Once

The registrar will offer it. It notifies HMRC, the DWP, the Passport Office, DVLA and the council in one step. It does not cover banks, private pensions, insurers or utilities.

6. Arrange the funeral

There is no deadline. Most funerals happen within one to three weeks, but longer is entirely normal, particularly where the Fiscal is involved or family are travelling from abroad.

7. Check what help is available

Funeral Support Payment can be paid straight to the funeral director. See our guide to Funeral Support Payment.

What can wait

Everything else. The estate, the house, the bank accounts, the clothes, the thank-you letters. None of it is urgent, whatever it feels like, and it will all still be there in a month.

How Scotland differs from England and Wales

  • You have eight days to register, not five.
  • You may register at any office in the country, not only the local one.
  • Sudden deaths go to the Procurator Fiscal, not a coroner.
  • There is no burial or cremation fee for anyone under 18.
  • Funeral Support Payment is the Scottish equivalent of the Funeral Expenses Payment, and is more generous.
  • Confirmation is what probate is called here.
